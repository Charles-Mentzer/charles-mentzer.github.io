Hi, my name is Charles Mentzer

I completed my PhD in Physics at the University of Missouri under the supervision of Prof. Aigen Li, where my dissertation focused on PAH emission and dust processing in protoplanetary disks and nearby galaxies. My current research continues to explore how small carbonaceous grains evolve from molecular clouds into the environments where planets ultimately form.

Currently I am a Postdoctoral Fellow at the Institute of Astronomy, National Tsing Hua University, working with Prof. Daniel Harsono on the chemical and physical evolution of protoplanetary disks. My research combines multi-wavelength observations, infrared spectroscopy, and physically motivated modeling to understand how dust, gas, and complex organic molecules evolve during the planet-forming stage of circumstellar disks.

My primary focus is on polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) and carbonaceous nanoparticles as tracers of disk structure, radiation environments, and chemical processing. Using data from JWST, ALMA, the VLT, and archival infrared observatories, I investigate how PAH emission varies with disk geometry, stellar UV radiation, and local physical conditions.

Scientific Outreach and Education:

I am deeply committed to making astronomy accessible to the public and to inspiring the next generation of scientists. During my PhD at the University of Missouri, I served as Outreach Coordinator for Laws Observatory, where I organized public observing nights, developed educational programming, and led outreach events for students, families, and community groups. These experiences strengthened my ability to communicate complex scientific ideas to audiences with diverse backgrounds.

I continue to be actively involved in science communication through mentorship of students interested in astronomy and data analysis. I believe that outreach is an essential part of scientific research: discoveries made with facilities such as JWST and ALMA have the greatest impact when they are shared beyond the research community and used to foster curiosity about the universe and the scientific process.
